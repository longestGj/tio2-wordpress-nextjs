Where should I start if I am comparing TiO₂ grades?+

How to Evaluate an Alternative Titanium Dioxide Grade

Technical GuideLast updated: 27 Aug 2026
Direct answer

Evaluate a titanium dioxide alternative grade through a controlled, staged comparison—not by matching a few powder properties. First define the current control and acceptance limits, then screen documents and powder data, run a one-variable same-formulation lab test, adjust the candidate where justified, complete a production-representative trial, and obtain finished-product approval.

Key conclusions

  • An alternative grade is a candidate for evaluation, not an assumed one-for-one substitute.
  • Powder and document data can help form a test hypothesis, but they cannot establish finished-system performance.
  • A same-formulation comparison shows the initial effect of changing the pigment; it does not show the candidate’s performance after candidate-specific optimization.
  • Production-representative processing and finished-product approval remain necessary before commercial adoption.

01

Start with the current control, not the candidate

A meaningful evaluation starts by defining what the current grade does in the actual system. Record the formulation, process settings, test methods, current results and acceptance limits before introducing a candidate. This turns “looks similar” into a controlled technical question.

Define the decision criteria in advance. Depending on the application, these may include appearance, opacity or tinting response, dispersion, viscosity or melt flow, processing stability, storage, finished-product performance and exposure-related durability. Rank the criteria by importance and identify any non-negotiable limits.

The candidate should then be evaluated against the current control under the same documented conditions. A change in result can be interpreted only when the formulation and process variables are controlled.

For background on why one reported property cannot determine a finished result, see Why TiO₂ Content Alone Does Not Determine Performance. Related screening factors include titanium dioxide oil absorption, CBU and titanium dioxide surface treatment.

Current grade→Candidate grade→Same formulation→Same process→Same test→Compare outcome

03

Build the same-formulation laboratory comparison

The first laboratory screen should change one main variable: the pigment grade. Keep the formula, raw-material sources, addition order, dispersion or compounding method, energy input, temperature and test procedure aligned with the current control.

Record practical observations as well as measured values. For liquid systems, note wetting, grind behavior, fineness, viscosity, flow, storage response and application appearance. For plastics and masterbatch, record feed behavior, dispersion, melt-flow response, extrusion conditions and finished-part appearance. For inks, maintain the same vehicle, milling conditions, printing method and drying conditions.

If the candidate misses an acceptance criterion, identify the mechanism to investigate before changing the formula. For example, a rheology shift may justify a controlled review of dispersant or binder balance; an appearance difference may require review of dispersion quality, film build, print conditions or loading. Do not change several variables at once, because the result will no longer show what caused the improvement or loss.

05

What the Comparison Means in Different Applications

Coatings

A coating comparison should distinguish between pigment effects and changes caused by dispersion, binder balance, pigment-volume position, application method, film build and cure. Compare the current control and candidate under matched conditions before considering any formulation adjustment. When exterior or other exposure-sensitive performance matters, include the relevant finished-film durability checks in the approval plan.

Plastics and masterbatch

In plastics and masterbatch, a candidate can affect dispersion, melt behavior, process stability and the appearance of the finished article. A powder comparison alone cannot show how the pigment will behave during compounding, extrusion, molding or conversion. Test under representative temperature and throughput conditions, then review the actual finished part or film.

Inks

Inks require control of the vehicle, milling process, filtration, printing method and drying conditions. A candidate that appears acceptable after milling may still need assessment for storage, transfer, print appearance and the relevant finished-print requirement. Match the intended print process before interpreting the result.

Other systems

Paper, film, functional materials and other systems need their own decision criteria. Start with the performance requirement, then define the process variables and finished-product tests that can demonstrate it. Do not assume that a scorecard developed for another application family answers the selection question.

06

When TDS Comparison Is No Longer Enough

When dispersion, rheology, opacity, gloss, processing or durability can change the result, a document comparison must continue into application testing.

Discuss the application before selecting a test candidate when the end-use exposure, process window, formulation limits or failure mode is unclear. The technical discussion is more useful when it includes:

  • Current grade details and the reason for the evaluation.
  • Formulation type, resin, binder or vehicle.
  • Pigment loading and relevant additive package.
  • Manufacturing process, equipment and operating conditions.
  • The observed failure mode or performance objective.
  • Acceptance limits, test method and target end-use exposure.

Apply the guidance

What This Means for Grade Replacement

  • Define the current control, acceptance limits and non-negotiable conditions before screening a candidate grade.
  • Keep the first laboratory formula and process matched so the initial pigment effect can be interpreted.
  • Treat candidate adjustment and production-representative validation as later stages, with finished-product acceptance required before adoption.

Common Mistakes When Comparing Titanium Dioxide Grades

01

Treating similar reported properties as proof of comparable formulation behavior.

02

Changing pigment, dispersant, loading and process settings in the same screen.

03

Comparing samples made with different equipment, energy input, temperatures or application conditions.

04

Stopping after an initial laboratory result without representative process validation.

05

Using only a visual comparison when rheology, melt flow, storage or finished performance is critical.

06

Defining approval criteria only after seeing the candidate result, instead of setting them in advance against the intended end-use requirements.

Practical validation

How to Move From Lab Screen to Finished-Product Approval

  1. 01

    Prepare the current formulation as the control and confirm that it gives an expected baseline result.

  2. 02

    Introduce the candidate at the same pigment loading and keep other formulation and process variables unchanged.

  3. 03

    Test the priority criteria defined at the start of the program, including relevant appearance, dispersion, process and finished-performance measures.

  4. 04

    Review the gap against the acceptance limits. If adjustment is technically justified, define a limited candidate-specific adjustment plan and document each change.

  5. 05

    Re-test the adjusted candidate against the same control and acceptance criteria.

  6. 06

    Move only credible candidates to a production-representative trial with documented operating conditions and quality checks.

  7. 07

    Complete finished-product and customer approval using the end-use requirements that govern the product.

Common technical questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Can powder data confirm that a titanium dioxide candidate will work in my formulation?+

No. Powder and document data can support initial screening, but the formulation, processing conditions and end-use requirements determine the final result. Use the data to define a controlled comparison, then validate the complete system.

Why should the first lab comparison keep the formula unchanged?+

A same-formulation comparison isolates the initial effect of changing the pigment. If several variables change at once, it becomes difficult to determine whether the candidate, formulation adjustment or process change caused the result.

When should a candidate-specific formulation adjustment be considered?+

Consider an adjustment only after the same-formulation screen identifies a clear, relevant gap and there is a defined technical reason to investigate it. Document the adjustment and compare it again with the current control under the same acceptance criteria.

Is a laboratory result enough before use in production?+

Usually, no. Laboratory work can support a decision to proceed, but representative production processing may reveal effects on throughput, stability, appearance or quality that were not visible at laboratory scale.

What information should be prepared for a technical discussion?+

Prepare the current grade details, formulation or resin/binder/vehicle, loading, process, failure mode, acceptance limits and end-use exposure. This information helps define a focused evaluation plan.

What Does Oil Absorption Mean in Titanium Dioxide?

Technical GuideLast updated: 27 Aug 2026
Direct answer

Oil absorption is the amount of a specified oil needed to reach a defined paste endpoint with a pigment under a stated test method, commonly reported as g oil per 100 g pigment. It is a method-bound indicator of powder–vehicle interaction, not a stand-alone prediction of finished-formula viscosity, binder demand, dispersant demand, or end-use performance.

01

Oil absorption is a test result, not a universal formulation answer

For titanium dioxide, oil absorption describes how much test oil is incorporated into a known mass of pigment until the procedure’s defined endpoint is reached. The reported result is commonly expressed as grams of oil per 100 grams of pigment.

The method matters. Test oil, mixing technique, endpoint definition, sample handling and reference practice can affect the result. A useful comparison therefore uses the same method, the same endpoint and, preferably, an agreed reference sample tested at the same time.

Oil absorption can help a formulator plan a screening program, particularly when vehicle balance and rheology are important. It does not, by itself, establish finished-ink viscosity, finished-paint viscosity, binder demand, dispersant demand, hiding, storage stability or print and film performance.

02

Why lower oil absorption is not always better

A lower value may be relevant when a formulation needs to manage vehicle demand or flow. It is not, however, a universal quality ranking.

Titanium dioxide grades are designed for different applications, surface treatments and formulation targets. A value should be interpreted with the grade’s intended application and the complete formulation in mind. Two values measured by different methods or endpoints should not be used to rank pigments.

Even when the method is aligned, the finished result depends on variables such as:

  • Vehicle or binder chemistry
  • Pigment and extender loading
  • Dispersant selection and dosage
  • Grinding energy and achieved fineness
  • Water, solvent or resin balance
  • Target pigment volume concentration (PVC)
  • Application method, drying or curing conditions

Treat oil absorption as a starting input for formulation work, then validate the complete system.

03

Oil absorption, viscosity and vehicle demand

Oil absorption may indicate how a pigment could interact with the vehicle during dispersion, but it is not a finished-formula viscosity measurement. A change in powder value may or may not translate into a meaningful viscosity or flow change after the formulation is adjusted.

For example, a formulation team may hypothesize that a pigment with a higher reported oil absorption will require changes to vehicle, binder or dispersant balance. That hypothesis should be tested under controlled conditions. Grinding, particle deagglomeration, dispersant response, extender package and temperature can all alter the practical result.

The same caution applies to dispersant demand. A powder result does not predict the optimum dispersant level. Use a dosage ladder to identify the practical balance among fineness, viscosity, flow, storage stability and finished performance.

04

Oil absorption in high-PVC coatings

PVC and critical pigment volume concentration (CPVC) describe relationships within a complete coating formulation, not within a titanium dioxide powder measurement. At high PVC, the balance among pigment, extender, binder and void structure can be especially important to film integrity and appearance.

A pigment’s oil absorption may be relevant when planning that balance, but it does not establish the formulation’s position relative to CPVC or predict dry hiding. In a high-PVC paint, evaluate the complete formulation for dry and wet hiding, rheology, porosity, cohesion, adhesion, scrub resistance and appearance.

05

How Oil Absorption Should Be Interpreted by Application

The following examples show why reported oil absorption values should be read as application-specific screening information, not as a performance hierarchy.

TP-I100 and TP-C200 are application-specific examples intended for different formulation contexts. Their reported values do not establish interchangeability, a universal preference for lower or higher oil absorption, or a prediction of finished-formula performance.

06

What to Check Before Comparing Two TiO₂ Grades

Before using oil absorption to guide a selection decision, ask:

  • Were both values measured by the same method and endpoint?
  • Was an agreed reference sample tested at the same time?
  • Are the pigments being evaluated in the same vehicle or binder system?
  • Has dispersant dosage been optimized separately for each candidate?
  • Are pigment loading, fineness and temperature controlled?
  • Has the finished ink or coating been tested against its actual acceptance criteria?

Apply the guidance

What This Means for Formulation Decisions

  • For ink trials, use the aligned powder comparison to plan vehicle balance, dispersion, fineness, viscosity, settling, redispersion and print-performance checks.
  • For high-PVC coatings, use oil absorption to plan binder, dispersant and rheology screening, then verify dry and wet hiding, film integrity, storage behavior and the formulation’s position relative to CPVC.
  • Do not transfer a TP-I100 or TP-C200 oil-absorption value into a preference, interchangeability claim or finished-formula prediction.

Practical validation

How to Validate Oil Absorption in Your Formulation

  1. 01

    Align the powder comparison: use the same test method, endpoint and sample handling, with an agreed reference sample tested at the same time.

  2. 02

    Fix the starting formulation: control vehicle or binder chemistry, pigment loading, extender package and temperature.

  3. 03

    Run an incremental dispersant ladder using the intended dispersion process; assess grind efficiency, fineness, viscosity and flow.

  4. 04

    Assess dispersion quality under comparable mixing energy and grind endpoint; check fineness, coarse particles or residue, and visual uniformity.

  5. 05

    Check storage stability under defined conditions and intervals; assess settling, redispersion, viscosity change and flow.

  6. 06

    Test the finished system under matched film or print conditions for hiding, appearance, adhesion, rub, scrub, drying, gloss and other application-relevant performance.

Common technical questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Does oil absorption measure finished-paint or finished-ink viscosity?+

No. It is a powder measurement made under a defined test method. Finished viscosity and flow depend on the complete formulation, dispersant response, dispersion process, temperature and other variables.

Can two oil absorption values be compared directly?+

Only when the method and endpoint are aligned. A simultaneous test against an agreed reference sample provides a stronger comparison than values produced at different times or under different procedures.

Does higher oil absorption establish dry hiding in a coating?+

No. Dry hiding must be measured in the finished coating at controlled film thickness and formulation conditions. Oil absorption is one formulation input and does not establish dry hiding on its own.

Does lower oil absorption mean lower dispersant demand?+

Not necessarily. Dispersant demand depends on the pigment, vehicle or binder, additive package, dispersion process and target performance. Use a controlled dosage ladder to determine the practical optimum.

How should oil absorption be used when formulating high-PVC paint?+

Use it to plan screening of binder balance, dispersant level, rheology and film integrity. Then verify dry and wet hiding, storage stability, porosity, cohesion, adhesion and scrub or washability as required.
